Population genetics studies how allele frequencies change within and between populations over time. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) connect genetic variants to phenotypes and diseases, forming the basis of precision medicine.

Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) describes the expected genotype frequencies in a population with no evolutionary forces acting. For a variant with allele frequencies p (reference) and q (alternative), expected genotype frequencies are: p² (homozygous ref), 2pq (heterozygous), q² (homozygous alt). Deviations from HWE can indicate selection, population stratification, genotyping errors, or inbreeding.

Key Population Genetics Concepts

Allele Frequency

The proportion of a specific allele in a population. If 80 of 200 chromosomes carry the T allele, frequency = 0.40. Minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01 = rare variant.

Genetic Drift

Random fluctuation of allele frequencies due to finite population size. Stronger in small populations. Founder effect and bottleneck are examples of extreme drift.

FST (Fixation Index)

Measures genetic differentiation between populations. FST = 0 means identical; FST = 1 means completely different. Human populations: FST ≈ 0.12 globally.

Linkage Disequilibrium (LD)

Non-random association between alleles at different loci. Nearby variants are in LD (inherited together). Measured by r². LD decays with physical distance due to recombination.

Population stratification in GWAS: If cases and controls come from different ancestral backgrounds, allele frequency differences due to ancestry (not disease) will create false associations. This is why GWAS always includes PCA components as covariates — to correct for population structure. Typically the top 10–20 PCs are included.

The null model that everything else is measured against

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium says that in a large, randomly mating population with no selection, mutation or migration, allele frequencies stay the same from generation to generation, and genotype frequencies follow from them:

p = frequency of allele A q = frequency of allele a p + q = 1 AA = p² Aa = 2pq aa = q² p² + 2pq + q² = 1 # Example: in 1,000 people, 360 AA, 480 Aa, 160 aa # p = (2×360 + 480) / 2000 = 0.6 q = 0.4 # Expected: AA = 360, Aa = 480, aa = 160 → this locus is in equilibrium

The value of the model is not that populations obey it. It is that departures from it are informative, and that in practice almost every departure a genomicist meets is a technical fault rather than biology.

Why HWE is a quality-control filter

In a GWAS, sites that deviate strongly from HWE are routinely removed. The reason is that genotyping errors produce characteristic deviations: a failing probe may call heterozygotes as homozygotes, giving far too few heterozygotes, while a probe hitting a duplicated region gives far too many. Conventions:

  • Test HWE in controls only. At a locus genuinely associated with disease, cases are expected to deviate, so testing them discards real signal.
  • Typical thresholds are p < 1×10−6 in controls, and a laxer p < 1×10−10 in cases.
  • Test within each ancestry group separately. Mixing populations with different allele frequencies creates an apparent heterozygote deficit (the Wahlund effect) at nearly every locus.

Seeing structure in your own data

# Standard quality control before any population analysis (PLINK 2) plink2 --vcf cohort.vcf.gz --make-bed --out raw plink2 --bfile raw \ --geno 0.02 \ # drop variants missing in >2% of samples --mind 0.02 \ # drop samples missing >2% of variants --maf 0.01 \ # drop very rare variants --hwe 1e-6 \ # drop variants far from equilibrium --make-bed --out qc # Prune correlated variants: structure analyses assume near-independence plink2 --bfile qc --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2 --out pruned plink2 --bfile qc --extract pruned.prune.in --pca 10 --out pca

Plotting the first two principal components separates samples by ancestry. The same components are then used as covariates in association testing, which is the standard way to stop ancestry differences producing false associations.

Check relatedness as well. Duplicated samples and close relatives break the independence that association tests assume. Estimate kinship (KING, or PLINK's --king-cutoff 0.0884 for second-degree relatives) and either remove one of each pair or move to a mixed model that accounts for relatedness.

GWAS tests each genetic variant across the genome for association with a phenotype (disease, height, drug response, etc.). It is a hypothesis-free approach — no prior knowledge of which genes are involved is needed. Since millions of variants are tested simultaneously, very stringent significance thresholds are required (p < 5×10⁻⁸ for genome-wide significance). GWAS results are visualised as Manhattan plots.

Interpreting GWAS Results

GWAS finds associations, not causation. A significant SNP is usually a tag SNP in LD with the true causal variant — not the causal variant itself. Fine-mapping (credible set analysis, SuSiE) is needed to narrow down the causal variant. Functional validation (CRISPR, reporter assays) is needed to prove causality. Most GWAS hits are in non-coding regions (enhancers, promoters), not in protein-coding exons.
Post-GWAS analysis: After finding significant loci, perform: (1) Fine-mapping — identify the most likely causal variant. (2) Colocalization (coloc, eCAVIAR) — test if a GWAS signal and an eQTL signal share the same causal variant. (3) Gene mapping — assign GWAS hits to likely target genes using eQTL data, chromatin interaction (Hi-C), and distance. (4) Pathway enrichment — test if GWAS hits are enriched in specific biological pathways. (5) Polygenic Risk Scores — combine effects of all variants to predict individual risk.

What a GWAS actually tests

For each variant in turn, a GWAS fits a regression of the phenotype on the genotype, coded as the number of effect alleles (0, 1 or 2), with covariates:

Quantitative trait: y = β₀ + β·genotype + covariates + ε (linear) Binary trait: logit(P) = β₀ + β·genotype + covariates (logistic) # Covariates almost always include: age, sex, genotyping batch, # and the first 10 principal components of ancestry

β is the effect size: change in the trait per copy of the allele, or the log odds ratio for a disease. The output is one p-value and one effect size per variant, for millions of variants.

Why the threshold is 5 × 10⁻⁸

Testing a million independent variants at p < 0.05 would give 50,000 false positives. The common human genome contains roughly one million independent blocks of linkage disequilibrium, so a Bonferroni correction gives 0.05 / 1,000,000 = 5 × 10−8. The threshold is conventional rather than exact, and studies of rare variants or of non-European populations with shorter LD blocks may need a stricter one.

The plots and what they say

Manhattan plot

Every variant by position and significance. Real associations appear as towers, because the causal variant drags its LD neighbours up with it. An isolated single dot with no supporting neighbours is usually a genotyping artefact.

Q-Q plot

Observed p-values against those expected by chance. The line should follow the diagonal and lift only at the far right. A line raised along its whole length means uncorrected structure or relatedness.

The accompanying number is lambda (genomic inflation), the ratio of the median observed test statistic to its expected value. Values between 1.0 and 1.05 are healthy; much above that, add ancestry components or move to a mixed model. In very large studies lambda rises with true polygenic signal too, so LD score regression is used to separate confounding from genuine polygenicity.

From a hit to a mechanism

A genome-wide significant association names a region, not a gene. Over 90% of hits fall outside coding sequence, in regulatory regions. The work after the scan is the harder half:

  1. Fine-mapping (SuSiE, FINEMAP) narrows the signal to a credible set of variants that could be causal.
  2. Colocalisation with eQTL data asks whether the same variant that affects the trait also affects the expression of a nearby gene.
  3. Functional annotation checks whether the credible-set variants sit in enhancers or open chromatin active in a relevant cell type.
  4. Experimental follow-up, such as a reporter assay or base editing, tests the candidate directly.

Effect sizes are small, and portability is limited. Typical odds ratios for common variants are 1.05 to 1.2, which is why sample sizes run to hundreds of thousands. Polygenic scores built in one population also predict poorly in another, because LD patterns and allele frequencies differ. Most published GWAS data come from European-ancestry cohorts, and this remains one of the field's largest open problems.
