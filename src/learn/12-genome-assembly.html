De novo genome assembly reconstructs complete chromosome sequences from millions of short or long sequencing reads — like assembling a jigsaw puzzle without the picture on the box. With the emergence of long-read technologies, telomere-to-telomere assemblies are now achievable for many organisms.

Genome assembly combines overlapping reads into longer contiguous sequences (contigs), then scaffolds contigs into chromosome-scale sequences. The approach depends fundamentally on read length: short reads (150 bp Illumina) cannot span repetitive elements, producing fragmented assemblies. Long reads (10–100 kb from PacBio HiFi or Oxford Nanopore) span most repeats, enabling near-complete assemblies.

Assembly Strategies

Short-read (de Bruijn graph)

Chops reads into k-mers, builds a graph where k-mers are nodes and overlaps are edges. Traverses the graph to produce contigs. Tools: SPAdes (small genomes, metagenomes), MEGAHIT (metagenomes). Good for bacteria, not for complex eukaryotes.

Long-read (overlap-layout-consensus)

Finds overlaps between all read pairs, constructs a layout graph, derives consensus sequence. Tools: hifiasm (PacBio HiFi — best), Flye (Nanopore/PacBio CLR). Produces chromosome-scale contigs for most genomes.

# ─── Bacterial genome (short reads, SPAdes) ─── mamba install -c bioconda spades quast spades.py -1 reads_R1.fastq.gz -2 reads_R2.fastq.gz \ -o spades_output/ -t 16 --careful # ─── Eukaryotic genome (PacBio HiFi, hifiasm) ─── mamba install -c bioconda hifiasm # Haplotype-resolved assembly (diploid) hifiasm -o asm -t 32 hifi_reads.fastq.gz # Convert GFA to FASTA awk '/^S/{print ">"$2"\n"$3}' asm.bp.p_ctg.gfa | fold > primary_assembly.fa # ─── Nanopore (Flye) ─── mamba install -c bioconda flye flye --nano-hq nanopore_reads.fastq.gz \ --out-dir flye_output/ --threads 32 --genome-size 500m # Polish Nanopore assembly with short reads (Illumina) mamba install -c bioconda pilon pilon --genome flye_output/assembly.fasta \ --frags illumina_aligned.bam --output polished
Assembly quality metrics: N50 = the contig length such that 50% of the assembly is in contigs of this size or larger. Higher N50 = more contiguous. L50 = how many contigs make up 50% of the assembly. Lower L50 = better. BUSCO = completeness assessment based on universal single-copy orthologs (aim for >95% complete). Always report all three.

Why assembly is hard

Assembly is the problem of reconstructing a text from short overlapping excerpts, where the text contains long passages repeated word for word. Those repeats are the whole difficulty. If a repeat is longer than your reads, no read spans it, and the assembler cannot tell how many copies there are or what order the surrounding regions come in. Roughly half of the human genome is repetitive, which is why short-read assemblies of large genomes stop at fragments.

The choice of k in a de Bruijn assembler is a trade-off: a small k gives better coverage of low-depth regions but tangles repeats; a large k resolves repeats but needs deeper, cleaner data. SPAdes sidesteps this by assembling with several values of k and combining the results.

What long reads changed

A PacBio HiFi read is 15 to 25 kb at over 99.9% accuracy, and a nanopore read can be hundreds of kb. Most repeats are shorter than that, so single reads span them. The practical consequences are large:

  • A bacterial genome from HiFi data routinely assembles into one circular contig per replicon.
  • Human assemblies reach contig N50 values in the tens of megabases, where short reads gave tens of kilobases.
  • Phasing becomes possible: the two parental copies of each chromosome can be assembled separately instead of being merged into one average sequence. Tools such as hifiasm produce such haplotype-resolved assemblies, especially with parental data (trio binning) or Hi-C.

A workflow that works today

# Check the data before assembling: genome size, heterozygosity, repeat content jellyfish count -C -m 21 -s 1G -t 8 reads.fq -o counts.jf jellyfish histo counts.jf > counts.histo # upload counts.histo to GenomeScope2 for the estimate # Bacterial genome from nanopore reads flye --nano-hq reads.fq.gz --out-dir flye_out --threads 16 # Eukaryotic genome from PacBio HiFi (add Hi-C for phasing) hifiasm -o asm -t 32 hifi_reads.fq.gz awk '/^S/{print ">"$2"\n"$3}' asm.bp.p_ctg.gfa > assembly.fasta # Polish nanopore assemblies (HiFi assemblies rarely need it) medaka_consensus -i reads.fq.gz -d assembly.fasta -o polished -t 8

Scaffolding, gaps and what "chromosome-level" means

Contigs are stretches of continuous sequence. Scaffolding orders and orients them using longer-range information (Hi-C contacts, optical maps, linked reads, or a related genome) and joins them with runs of N characters standing for gaps of estimated length. A "chromosome-level" assembly has scaffolds the size of chromosomes; it may still contain many gaps. A telomere-to-telomere assembly has none. When you download a genome, check the number of scaffolds, the number of gaps, and whether the sequence is phased, before treating it as complete.

Contamination is normal in draft assemblies. Bacterial, fungal and adapter sequence slips into most projects. Screen with BlobTools (which plots contigs by GC content, coverage and taxonomy) or NCBI's FCS tool before submission, and remove organellar contigs into their own files.

An assembly is only useful if it is correct and complete. Quality assessment uses two complementary approaches: contiguity statistics (N50, total length, number of contigs) measured by QUAST, and gene completeness measured by BUSCO (Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs), which checks whether expected genes are present and intact.

# ─── QUAST — contiguity statistics ─── mamba install -c bioconda quast quast assembly.fa -o quast_output/ -t 8 # If you have a reference genome for comparison: quast assembly.fa -r reference.fa -g genes.gff -o quast_output/ -t 8 # Key outputs: N50, L50, total length, # contigs, GC%, misassemblies # ─── BUSCO — gene completeness ─── mamba install -c bioconda busco # Choose the right lineage dataset for your organism: busco --list-datasets # Shows all available lineages # Run BUSCO busco -i assembly.fa -o busco_output -m genome \ -l mammalia_odb10 -c 16 # Interpretation: # C: >95% = excellent assembly # C: 90-95% = good # C: <80% = significant genes missing (check lineage choice) # D: >5% = possible contamination or collapsed haplotypes
Merqury is a reference-free assembly evaluation tool that uses k-mer counts from raw reads to assess completeness, accuracy, and phasing of haplotype-resolved assemblies. It is now the gold standard for HiFi assemblies.

Three questions, three kinds of metric

Assembly quality is not one number. Ask three separate questions, and use a different tool for each:

Is it contiguous?

N50, L50, number of contigs, longest contig, total length. QUAST reports all of these. They say how broken the assembly is, and nothing about whether it is correct.

Is it complete?

BUSCO: the fraction of a set of genes expected in single copy in this lineage that can be found. Also compare the assembly size with the GenomeScope estimate.

Is it accurate?

Merqury: compares k-mers in the reads with k-mers in the assembly, giving a base-level quality value (QV) and a k-mer completeness, without needing a reference.

Reading N50 correctly

N50 is the length of the contig at which half the assembly is contained in contigs of that length or longer. It is useful but easy to game: joining two contigs incorrectly raises N50 and makes the assembly worse. Two companions make it honest:

  • NG50 uses the expected genome size instead of the assembled size, so two assemblies of different total length can be compared.
  • NGA50 (QUAST, with a reference) breaks contigs at misassemblies first. A large gap between N50 and NGA50 means many wrong joins.

# Contiguity quast.py assembly.fasta -o quast_out --threads 8 # Completeness (pick the closest lineage; --auto-lineage if unsure) busco -i assembly.fasta -l vertebrata_odb10 -m genome -o busco_out -c 8 # Base accuracy, reference-free, from the reads themselves meryl count k=21 reads.fq.gz output reads.meryl merqury.sh reads.meryl assembly.fasta merqury_out # QV 40 = 1 error in 10,000 bases; QV 50 = 1 in 100,000

Benchmarks to judge against

  • Bacterial, HiFi or good nanopore: one contig per replicon, BUSCO complete above 98%, QV above 50.
  • Vertebrate, HiFi plus Hi-C: contig N50 in the tens of Mb, scaffold N50 at chromosome scale, BUSCO above 95%, QV above 40 (the standard set by the Vertebrate Genomes Project).
  • Illumina-only draft of a large genome: contig N50 in the tens of kb, BUSCO 85 to 95%. Usable for gene content, not for structural work.
Report all three. An assembly described only by its N50 tells a reader almost nothing. "Contig N50 18.4 Mb, BUSCO 97.2% complete (1.1% duplicated), QV 52" is a complete description in one line.

After assembling a genome, the next step is annotation — finding where the genes are and what they encode. Gene prediction uses ab initio methods (statistical models trained on known gene structures), homology-based methods (aligning known proteins or transcripts), and evidence-based methods (using RNA-seq data from the same organism). Modern pipelines combine all three approaches.

Annotation Pipeline

# ─── Prokaryotic annotation (fast, automated) ─── mamba install -c bioconda prokka prokka assembly.fa --outdir annotation/ --prefix my_organism \ --kingdom Bacteria --genus Streptomyces --cpus 16 # Output: .gff (gene coordinates), .faa (protein sequences), # .ffn (gene nucleotide sequences), .gbk (GenBank format) # ─── Eukaryotic annotation (more complex) ─── # Step 1: Mask repeats (RepeatMasker) mamba install -c bioconda repeatmasker RepeatMasker -species "homo sapiens" -pa 16 -xsmall assembly.fa # Step 2: Align RNA-seq evidence (HISAT2 → StringTie) hisat2 -x genome_index -1 rnaseq_R1.fq.gz -2 rnaseq_R2.fq.gz -p 16 | \ samtools sort -O bam -o rnaseq_aligned.bam stringtie rnaseq_aligned.bam -o transcripts.gtf # Step 3: Run BRAKER2/3 (combines ab initio + RNA-seq + protein evidence) # BRAKER3 (recommended — integrates GeneMark + AUGUSTUS + protein hints) braker.pl --genome=assembly.fa.masked \ --bam=rnaseq_aligned.bam \ --prot_seq=proteins_from_related_species.fa \ --softmasking --threads 16 # Step 4: Functional annotation (assign GO terms, domains, pathways) mamba install -c bioconda interproscan interproscan.sh -i predicted_proteins.faa -f tsv -dp -goterms -pa
Repeat masking is essential: Eukaryotic genomes can be 40–80% repetitive. Without masking, gene predictors will predict thousands of spurious genes in transposable elements. Always run RepeatMasker before gene prediction. Use soft-masking (lowercase, not N-replacement) so gene predictors can still use the sequence information.

Structural and functional annotation are different jobs

Structural annotation decides where the genes are: the coordinates of exons, introns, start and stop codons, and alternative transcripts. Functional annotation decides what those genes do, by transferring information from characterised homologues and domain databases. The first is a prediction problem on your genome; the second is a search problem against existing knowledge.

Three sources of evidence

EvidenceStrengthWeakness
RNA-seq from the same organismDirect proof of transcription and of exact splice sitesMisses genes not expressed in the tissues sampled

Modern pipelines combine all three. BRAKER3 trains GeneMark and AUGUSTUS using RNA-seq and protein evidence together; MAKER weighs evidence tracks and produces a consensus; Funannotate wraps the whole process for fungi and small eukaryotes. For prokaryotes the problem is far easier, because genes are dense and rarely spliced: Prokka or Bakta annotate a bacterial genome in minutes.

Repeats first

Transposable elements carry their own open reading frames, and an unmasked genome yields thousands of spurious "genes" that are really transposon fragments. Annotation therefore starts with repeat identification: build a repeat library for the species with RepeatModeler, then soft-mask the genome with RepeatMasker (repeats in lowercase). Soft masking lets the predictor avoid starting a gene inside a repeat while still allowing an exon to extend through one.

Checking an annotation

  • BUSCO in protein mode on the predicted proteins. A genome that scored 97% complete but whose annotation scores 80% has a problem in the annotation, not the assembly.
  • Gene count and structure against close relatives. A plant with 80,000 predicted genes, or a vertebrate whose average gene has one exon, signals over-prediction or a frame problem.
  • Support per model. What fraction of genes is backed by RNA-seq or protein alignment? Models supported by nothing but the predictor deserve a lower-confidence flag.
  • Look at a browser. Load the GFF3 with the RNA-seq coverage in IGV or JBrowse and inspect twenty genes by eye. Systematic errors are usually obvious within minutes.

GFF3 in practice

Annotations are distributed as GFF3 or GTF: one feature per line, with coordinates, a type (gene, mRNA, exon, CDS) and attributes. The part that causes most trouble is the parent-child hierarchy, where each exon points to an mRNA and each mRNA to a gene through ID and Parent attributes. Tools disagree about details, so validate the file (gff3_QC from GFF3toolkit, or AGAT) before handing it to a counting tool or a browser; a silently malformed GFF3 is a common cause of zero counts in an RNA-seq run.

Naming genes: "transferred from the best BLAST hit" is a weak basis for a name, and errors propagate between databases. Prefer evidence from domain databases (InterPro, Pfam) and orthology (OrthoFinder, eggNOG), state the method in your methods section, and keep a stable identifier of your own alongside any transferred name.
