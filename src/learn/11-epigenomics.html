Epigenetics studies heritable changes in gene activity that do not involve changes to the DNA sequence. These modifications — histone marks, chromatin accessibility, and DNA methylation — control which genes are on or off in each cell type. Epigenomic assays reveal the regulatory landscape of the genome.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) identifies where a specific protein (transcription factor or modified histone) binds across the genome. The protein-DNA complexes are cross-linked with formaldehyde, chromatin is sheared into 200–500 bp fragments, an antibody pulls down fragments bound by the target protein, and the bound DNA is sequenced. Peaks in the sequencing signal reveal binding sites.

ChIP-seq Analysis Pipeline

Always use an input control. ChIP-seq without a control (input DNA or IgG pulldown) will produce false peaks at open chromatin regions and repetitive elements. The control captures background signal, enabling the peak caller to distinguish real binding from noise. No control = no publication.
CUT&RUN / CUT&Tag: Modern alternatives to ChIP-seq that require far fewer cells (100–1,000 vs millions), have lower background, and are cheaper. CUT&Tag is especially popular — it uses a protein A-Tn5 fusion to cut and tag DNA near the antibody target. Analysis is similar to ChIP-seq but use SEACR instead of MACS2 for peak calling.

What the experiment measures

ChIP-seq answers one question: where on the genome was a given protein sitting when the cells were fixed? Cells are treated with formaldehyde, which freezes proteins onto the DNA they touch. The chromatin is then broken into fragments of a few hundred base pairs, an antibody pulls down the fragments carrying the protein of interest, the links are reversed, and the released DNA is sequenced. Positions where many fragments accumulate are the places the protein occupied.

Knowing which shape to expect decides the depth you buy and the peak caller you use. Running a broad mark through a narrow caller gives a scatter of fragmentary peaks instead of one domain.

Controls are not optional

Sequencing coverage is never flat. Open chromatin shears more easily, repeats attract mapping errors, and copy-number differences change the local read density. All of this produces hills that look like peaks. The control removes them:

  • Input: chromatin taken from the same sample before the antibody step. This is the standard control and the one to use unless you have a reason not to.
  • IgG: a pull-down with a non-specific antibody. It measures antibody background but usually yields very little DNA, so the library is low-complexity.
  • Sequence the control at least as deeply as the ChIP sample, from the same batch of cells.

The ENCODE blacklist is a list of regions that give extreme signal in every experiment, mostly repeats and assembly artefacts. Removing them is one of the cheapest quality improvements available.

Making sense of the peaks

  • Annotate them relative to genes (ChIPseeker, HOMER): what fraction sit at promoters, in introns, or far from any gene?
  • Find motifs (MEME-ChIP, HOMER). For a transcription factor, recovering its known motif as the top enriched sequence is the strongest evidence that the experiment worked.
  • Compare conditions with DiffBind or csaw, which test read counts in a consensus peak set the same way DESeq2 tests genes.
  • Link to expression: peaks alone show binding, not effect. Pair them with RNA-seq from the same system to see which bound genes actually change.
The antibody is the experiment. Most failed ChIP-seq is a failed antibody. Use a ChIP-validated lot, and where possible confirm with a tagged version of the protein (CUT&RUN or CUT&Tag with a tag antibody), or with a knockout sample as a negative control.
Newer alternatives: CUT&RUN and CUT&Tag cut the DNA where the antibody binds instead of fragmenting everything. They need far fewer cells and far fewer reads (3 to 8 million), with much lower background. The analysis is similar, but peak callers are run with settings suited to the sparser background (SEACR, or MACS3 without a control).

ATAC-seq (Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing) maps open chromatin regions genome-wide. It uses the Tn5 transposase, which preferentially inserts sequencing adapters into accessible (open) chromatin, avoiding tightly packed nucleosome-occupied regions. Open chromatin corresponds to active regulatory elements — promoters, enhancers, and transcription factor binding sites.

Fragment size distribution: A good ATAC-seq library shows a characteristic nucleosomal ladder pattern: a peak at ~200 bp (nucleosome-free), ~400 bp (mono-nucleosome), ~600 bp (di-nucleosome). If you see only the ~200 bp peak, your transposition worked well. If the distribution is broad without clear peaks, the experiment may have had too many or too few Tn5 insertions.

How the Tn5 trick works

Tn5 transposase does two jobs in one reaction: it cuts DNA and ligates sequencing adapters onto the cut ends. This is called tagmentation. In ATAC-seq the enzyme is added to intact nuclei, so it can only reach DNA that is not wrapped around a nucleosome or covered by a bound protein. Reads therefore mark accessible chromatin: promoters, active enhancers, and sites held open by transcription factors. The whole protocol takes a few hours from 50,000 nuclei, with no antibody and no crosslinking.

Fragment sizes carry information

A good ATAC-seq library shows a clear ladder in its fragment-size distribution, and this plot is the fastest quality check available:

  • Under 100 bp: nucleosome-free fragments, from stretches of naked accessible DNA. These carry the sharpest signal.
  • About 180 to 250 bp: fragments spanning one nucleosome.
  • About 350 to 500 bp: two nucleosomes, and so on, with each step fading.

The periodicity of roughly 150 to 200 bp reflects the size of a nucleosome plus its linker. A smooth distribution with no ladder means the nuclei were over-digested or the chromatin was degraded.

The two problems specific to ATAC-seq

Mitochondrial reads

Mitochondrial DNA has no nucleosomes, so Tn5 attacks it freely. In early protocols it took 20 to 80% of all reads. Omni-ATAC reduces this with a detergent wash; whatever the protocol, remove chrM reads before counting anything, and judge depth by the reads that remain.

The 9 bp offset

Tn5 binds as a dimer and inserts adapters 9 bp apart. For base-resolution work such as footprinting, shift reads on the plus strand by +4 and on the minus strand by −5 so the cut sites line up exactly.

# Align, then remove mitochondrial and duplicate reads bowtie2 -x genome -1 atac_R1.fq.gz -2 atac_R2.fq.gz -X 2000 -p 8 \ | samtools sort -o atac.bam samtools index atac.bam samtools idxstats atac.bam | cut -f1,3 # how many reads are chrM? samtools view -b -f 2 -q 30 atac.bam $(samtools idxstats atac.bam | cut -f1 | grep -v -e chrM -e '\*') > atac.nochrM.bam picard MarkDuplicates I=atac.nochrM.bam O=atac.dedup.bam M=dup.txt REMOVE_DUPLICATES=true # Fragment-size distribution (the key QC plot) bamPEFragmentSize -b atac.dedup.bam -hist fragment_sizes.png # Peaks: no input control exists, so call against the local background macs3 callpeak -t atac.dedup.bam -f BAMPE -g hs -n atac --outdir peaks/ \ -q 0.01 --nomodel --shift -75 --extsize 150

The TSS enrichment score compares read density at transcription start sites with a flanking background. Promoters are accessible in nearly every cell type, so a flat TSS profile means the experiment did not work, whatever the peak count says.

What you can ask of the data

  • Differential accessibility: count reads in a consensus peak set and test with DESeq2 or csaw, exactly as for gene counts.
  • Motif enrichment: which transcription factor motifs are over-represented in the regions that opened or closed? chromVAR scores this per sample, HOMER and MEME per peak set.
  • Footprinting: a bound factor protects a few base pairs from Tn5, leaving a dip inside an otherwise open region. TOBIAS and HINT-ATAC detect these, but only with deep data.
  • Linking enhancers to genes: accessibility alone does not say which gene a distal peak controls. Correlate with expression across samples, or use Hi-C or similar contact data.
ATAC-seq or ChIP-seq? ATAC-seq shows where chromatin is open, without naming the protein responsible. ChIP-seq, CUT&RUN and CUT&Tag show where one named protein sits. A common pairing is ATAC-seq to find the regulatory regions that change, motif analysis to suggest which factors act there, and a targeted CUT&RUN to confirm that one factor.

DNA methylation — the addition of a methyl group to the 5th carbon of cytosine (5mC) — is the most studied epigenetic modification. In mammals, it occurs predominantly at CpG dinucleotides. Methylation of gene promoters generally silences transcription. It plays critical roles in development, X-chromosome inactivation, genomic imprinting, and cancer (where aberrant methylation is a hallmark).

Detection Methods

WGBS

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing. Gold standard. Single-CpG resolution across entire genome. Expensive — needs 10–15× coverage. Bisulfite converts unmethylated C→U; methylated C is protected.

RRBS

Reduced Representation BS. MspI digestion enriches CpG-rich regions. 1–10% of genome, covers most CpG islands. Much cheaper than WGBS.

Methylation Arrays

Illumina EPIC array (850K CpGs). Cheapest per-sample. Good for clinical studies. Limited to pre-selected CpGs. EPIC v2 covers ~930K sites.

Interpreting methylation: CpG island methylation at promoters = gene silencing. Gene body methylation = correlated with active transcription (paradoxically). Enhancer methylation = generally low at active enhancers. In cancer, look for promoter hypermethylation of tumour suppressors and global hypomethylation (genomic instability). DMRs (Differentially Methylated Regions) are typically more biologically meaningful than individual CpG changes — use tools like dmrseq or DSS for DMR calling.

What is being measured, and where

DNA methylation in mammals is a methyl group on the 5-carbon of cytosine, almost always in a CpG dinucleotide. Most of the genome's CpGs are methylated. The exceptions matter: CpG islands, short CG-rich stretches found at about 60% of human promoters, are usually unmethylated, and methylation there is associated with a silenced gene. In plants, and in mammalian stem cells and brain, methylation also occurs in CHG and CHH contexts, which is why alignment tools report the three contexts separately.

Reading the result

Methylation at a site is reported as a beta value: the proportion of reads at that cytosine that stayed C.

beta = methylated reads / (methylated + unmethylated reads) # 0.0 every copy unmethylated # 0.5 half the copies methylated (or one allele of two) # 1.0 every copy methylated

Because beta values are bounded between 0 and 1, their variance depends on the mean, which breaks ordinary linear models. Many tools therefore use the M value, log2(beta / (1 − beta)), for statistics, and report beta values for interpretation.

Why the analysis is different

After conversion the two DNA strands are no longer complementary, and most Cs have become Ts. A normal aligner fails. Tools such as Bismark handle this by converting both the reads and the reference entirely to Ts in silico, aligning in that reduced alphabet, and then restoring the original bases to score each cytosine.

# Prepare the converted reference once per genome bismark_genome_preparation --verbose /ref/GRCh38/ # Align, remove duplicates, then extract per-cytosine methylation bismark --genome /ref/GRCh38/ -1 sample_R1.fq.gz -2 sample_R2.fq.gz -p 4 deduplicate_bismark --paired sample_R1_bismark_bt2_pe.bam bismark_methylation_extractor --gzip --bedGraph --comprehensive \ sample_R1_bismark_bt2_pe.deduplicated.bam # The report gives the conversion rate: check it before anything else bismark2report

The checks that decide whether the data are usable

  • Conversion rate. Unmethylated lambda DNA is spiked in; because it has no methylation, any C still read as C is a conversion failure. Expect above 99%. At 97% conversion, a genuinely unmethylated site appears 3% methylated, which is enough to invent small differences.
  • Coverage per site. A beta value from 3 reads is almost meaningless. Require at least 5 to 10 reads per cytosine in every sample before testing it.
  • M-bias plot. Methylation should not depend on the position within the read. A bias at the start or end means the first or last few bases should be trimmed.
  • Mapping rate. Bisulfite libraries map less well than normal ones; 50 to 70% is typical, not a fault.
  • Cell composition. In blood or tissue, a change in the mix of cell types shifts methylation genome-wide and mimics a biological effect. Estimate and adjust for cell proportions (for example with the Houseman method) in any human cohort study.

Finding differences

Single sites are noisy, so most studies look for differentially methylated regions (DMRs): runs of neighbouring CpGs that change together. Tools in common use are methylKit and DSS (for sequencing data) and limma or minfi (for arrays). Report the size of the change as well as its p-value: a statistically significant shift of 3% in beta is rarely interesting, whereas a 30% shift across a promoter usually is.

Standard bisulfite cannot tell 5mC from 5hmC. Both survive conversion and read as C. Distinguishing them needs oxidative bisulfite (oxBS-seq), TAB-seq, or a nanopore model trained on both. This matters most in brain and stem cells, where hydroxymethylation is abundant.
