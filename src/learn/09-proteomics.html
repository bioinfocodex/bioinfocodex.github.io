Proteomics studies the entire complement of proteins in a cell, tissue, or organism. Unlike the genome (static), the proteome is dynamic — it changes with conditions, time, and cell type. Mass spectrometry is the workhorse technology for large-scale protein identification and quantification.

Mass spectrometry (MS) measures the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of ionised molecules. In proteomics, proteins are digested into peptides (typically with trypsin), separated by liquid chromatography (LC), ionised, and then measured in the mass spectrometer. The combination — LC-MS/MS — is the standard workflow for identifying and quantifying thousands of proteins in a single experiment.

The LC-MS/MS Workflow

A typical bottom-up proteomics experiment follows these steps: (1) Protein extraction — lyse cells, solubilise proteins. (2) Digestion — trypsin cleaves after lysine (K) and arginine (R), producing peptides of 7–35 amino acids. (3) Separation — reverse-phase liquid chromatography separates peptides by hydrophobicity over a 60–120 minute gradient. (4) Ionisation — electrospray ionisation (ESI) converts peptides into gas-phase ions. (5) MS1 scan — measures intact peptide masses. (6) MS2 scan — isolates individual peptide ions and fragments them (HCD or CID). The fragmentation pattern reveals the amino acid sequence.

Key Instrument Types

Orbitrap (Thermo)

High resolution (120,000–500,000), high mass accuracy (<2 ppm). Gold standard for discovery proteomics. Exploris 480, Q Exactive series.

TOF (Bruker timsTOF)

Time-of-flight with trapped ion mobility (TIMS). Very fast scan rates. DIA-PASEF enables deep proteome coverage. Excellent for clinical applications.

Triple Quadrupole (QQQ)

Lower resolution but extremely sensitive and quantitative. Used for targeted proteomics (MRM/SRM) — measuring specific proteins with high precision.

MALDI-TOF

Matrix-assisted laser desorption. Used for intact protein profiling, tissue imaging (MALDI-MSI), and microbial identification (Bruker Biotyper).

DDA vs DIA Acquisition

DDA (Data-Dependent)

Selects the top N most intense ions from MS1 for fragmentation. Simple, well-established. Stochastic — may miss low-abundance peptides. Less reproducible between runs.

DIA (Data-Independent)

Fragments ALL ions in wide m/z windows systematically. No selection bias. More reproducible and comprehensive. Requires spectral libraries or library-free tools (DIA-NN). Now the preferred method.

Recommended
Modern trend: DIA with tools like DIA-NN or Spectronaut is rapidly replacing DDA for most applications. DIA-NN can identify 8,000–10,000 proteins from a single human cell line in a 90-minute gradient, with excellent quantitative reproducibility.

What the instrument actually measures

A mass spectrometer never measures mass directly. It measures the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of ions. Electrospray ionisation adds protons to a peptide, so a peptide of neutral mass M carrying z protons appears at:

m/z = (M + z × 1.00728) / z # Example: a peptide with M = 1,500.00 Da z = 1 → m/z 1501.01 z = 2 → m/z 751.01 z = 3 → m/z 501.01

Tryptic peptides are usually seen at charge 2+ or 3+. The charge is read from the isotope pattern: because about 1.1% of carbon is the heavier 13C, every peptide gives a small ladder of peaks, and the spacing between them is 1/z (0.5 for 2+, 0.33 for 3+).

Mass accuracy is quoted in parts per million: ppm = (observed − theoretical) / theoretical × 1,000,000. A 5 ppm error on a peptide at m/z 750 is only 0.004.

Fragmentation: b and y ions

In MS2 the selected peptide is broken by collision with gas (CID or HCD). The peptide bond breaks, giving two families of fragments: b ions contain the N-terminus and y ions contain the C-terminus. The mass difference between neighbouring peaks in a series equals one amino acid, which is how a sequence is read from a spectrum. A gentler method, ETD, produces c and z ions and keeps fragile modifications such as phosphorylation attached, so it is used when the exact modified residue matters.

Why trypsin?

  • It cuts after lysine (K) and arginine (R), except when the next residue is proline, so the cut sites are predictable.
  • The peptides are mostly 7 to 25 residues long, which is the range that flies and fragments well.
  • Every peptide ends in a basic residue, which holds a proton at the C-terminus and gives strong y-ion series.

After acquiring MS/MS spectra, the key bioinformatics task is identifying which peptides (and therefore proteins) generated them. This is done by matching experimental spectra against theoretical spectra predicted from a protein sequence database. The process is called database searching — and it is conceptually similar to BLAST, but for mass spectra instead of sequences.

How Database Search Works

(1) Take each experimental MS2 spectrum. (2) In silico digest all proteins from a reference database (e.g., UniProt human) with trypsin. (3) For each candidate peptide whose mass matches the precursor mass (within tolerance), predict the theoretical fragmentation spectrum. (4) Score the match between experimental and theoretical spectra. (5) Apply statistical validation (FDR control at 1% using target-decoy approach).

Major Search Engines

MaxQuant

Free, widely used. Built-in Andromeda search engine. Excellent for label-free and SILAC. GUI-based. Standard in academic proteomics.

MSFragger / FragPipe

Ultrafast open search. 100× faster than traditional engines. Integrated in FragPipe with downstream analysis. Excellent for PTM discovery.

DIA-NN

Deep neural network for DIA data. Library-free mode generates in silico spectral libraries. State-of-the-art sensitivity and speed.

Target-Decoy FDR: All search engines use a decoy database (reversed or shuffled sequences) to estimate false discovery rate. At 1% FDR: for every 100 identified proteins, ~1 is expected to be wrong. Always use 1% FDR at both PSM (peptide-spectrum match) and protein level. Never relax this to 5%.

How a database search works, step by step

  1. Digest in silico. Every protein in the FASTA file is cut by the rules of your enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages.
  2. Filter by precursor mass. For each spectrum, only peptides whose mass matches the measured precursor within the tolerance (for example 10 ppm) are kept as candidates.
  3. Predict fragments. The b and y ions of each candidate are calculated.
  4. Score. The predicted fragments are compared with the observed peaks. The best candidate becomes a peptide-spectrum match (PSM).
  5. Control errors. A decoy database decides which scores can be trusted.

The target-decoy method

A reversed or shuffled copy of every protein is added to the database. Decoy peptides cannot be in the sample, so any spectrum that matches a decoy is a known false match. Because a wrong match is equally likely to hit a target or a decoy, the number of decoys above a score threshold estimates the number of false targets above it:

FDR ≈ (decoy matches above the threshold) / (target matches above the threshold) # 10,000 target PSMs and 100 decoy PSMs pass → FDR ≈ 1%

FDR is applied at three levels, and all three matter: PSM, peptide and protein. Errors accumulate at the protein level, because one wrong peptide is enough to report a wrong protein. That is why reliable results require a 1% protein-level FDR, and many labs also require two peptides per protein.

The protein inference problem

Mass spectrometry identifies peptides, not proteins. A peptide that occurs in several proteins (isoforms, paralogues) cannot say which one was present. Search engines therefore report protein groups: sets of proteins that cannot be told apart with the peptides found. Shared peptides are usually given to the group with the most other evidence (the "razor" rule). When you see a gene with several isoforms listed in one row, this is the reason.

Checking a search before you trust it

  • Identification rate: 30 to 60% of MS2 spectra identified is normal for DDA. Much lower suggests a wrong database, wrong tolerances or a poor run.
  • Missed cleavages: more than about 20 to 25% of peptides with a missed cleavage points to incomplete digestion.
  • Mass error: the distribution should be narrow and centred on zero ppm.
  • Contaminants: a sample dominated by keratin means handling contamination.
Search engines in common use: Andromeda (inside MaxQuant), MSFragger (inside FragPipe), Comet, Sage and Mascot for DDA; DIA-NN and Spectronaut for DIA. FragPipe, MaxQuant and DIA-NN are free for academic use.

Quantitative proteomics measures how much of each protein is present, enabling comparison between conditions. There are two main strategies: label-free quantification (LFQ), which compares ion intensities directly, and labelling methods (TMT, SILAC), which use chemical or metabolic tags to multiplex samples.

Quantification Strategies

Label-Free (LFQ)

Compare MS1 peptide intensities across runs. Simple, no extra chemistry. Requires careful normalisation. Best with DIA. MaxLFQ algorithm in MaxQuant.

Most common
TMT/iTRAQ

Chemical labels with same mass but different fragmentation reporters. Multiplex up to 18 samples in one run (TMTpro 18-plex). Reduces missing values. Higher throughput.

SILAC

Metabolic labelling — grow cells with heavy amino acids (13C-Lys, 13C-Arg). Most accurate quantification. Only works for cell culture. Not for tissues/clinical samples.

Statistical Analysis with R

# ─── Differential protein abundance analysis ─── # Using limma (same package used for microarrays — works great for proteomics) library(limma) library(tidyverse) # Load MaxQuant proteinGroups.txt or DIA-NN pg_matrix pg <- read.delim("proteinGroups.txt") # Filter: remove contaminants, reverse hits, only identified by site pg <- pg[pg$Reverse != "+" & pg$Potential.contaminant != "+" & pg$Only.identified.by.site != "+", ] # Extract LFQ intensity columns and log2-transform lfq <- pg[, grep("^LFQ.intensity.", names(pg))] lfq[lfq == 0] <- NA # Replace 0 with NA mat <- log2(as.matrix(lfq)) # Filter: keep proteins with values in at least 70% of samples per group # (adjust based on your design) # Imputation: replace missing values (MinProb method — common) # Or use DEqMS / msqrob2 for more sophisticated handling # Differential analysis with limma group <- factor(c("Control","Control","Control","Treated","Treated","Treated")) design <- model.matrix(~ 0 + group) colnames(design) <- levels(group) fit <- lmFit(mat, design) contrast <- makeContrasts(Treated - Control, levels = design) fit2 <- contrasts.fit(fit, contrast) fit3 <- eBayes(fit2) results <- topTable(fit3, number = Inf, sort.by = "P") sig <- results[results$adj.P.Val < 0.05 & abs(results$logFC) > 1, ] cat(nrow(sig), "significantly changed proteins (|logFC|>1, FDR<0.05)\n")
Missing values in proteomics: Unlike RNA-seq, proteomics data has many missing values (10–30% typically). These can be MCAR (missing completely at random — stochastic), MNAR (missing not at random — protein is below detection limit), or a mixture. Imputation strategy matters: use MinProb for MNAR, kNN for MCAR. Better yet, use statistical methods that handle missingness directly: DEqMS, msqrob2, or proDA.

Ratio compression is the main trap with TMT. Other peptides that are co-isolated with the target contribute their own reporter ions, pulling every ratio towards 1:1. A true four-fold change may be reported as two-fold. Measuring reporters at the MS3 level (SPS-MS3) reduces the problem at the cost of speed.

From intensities to a result table

  1. Remove contaminants, decoy hits and proteins identified only by a modification site.
  2. Log2-transform the intensities. Intensities span five or more orders of magnitude and are roughly normal only after the log.
  3. Normalise so that the samples are comparable, most simply by aligning the medians. This assumes most proteins do not change.
  4. Filter by missing values, for example keeping proteins measured in at least 70% of the samples of one group.
  5. Impute what is still missing, choosing the method by the reason for missingness (below).
  6. Test each protein with a moderated t-test (limma) or a mixed model (MSstats), then correct for multiple testing with Benjamini-Hochberg.

Missing values are not all the same

Missing not at random (MNAR)

The protein is below the detection limit. This is the usual case when a protein is absent from one condition. Impute with small values, for example drawn from a normal distribution shifted down by 1.8 standard deviations (the Perseus default).

Missing at random (MAR)

The peptide was present but was not picked for fragmentation in that run. Impute from similar proteins or samples, for example with k-nearest neighbours.

# Differential abundance with limma (R) # mat: log2 intensities, proteins in rows, samples in columns library(limma) group <- factor(c("ctrl","ctrl","ctrl","treat","treat","treat")) design <- model.matrix(~ 0 + group) colnames(design) <- levels(group) fit <- lmFit(mat, design) fit2 <- eBayes(contrasts.fit(fit, makeContrasts(treat - ctrl, levels = design))) res <- topTable(fit2, number = Inf, adjust.method = "BH") # Significant proteins: adjusted p below 0.05 and at least 1.5-fold change sig <- subset(res, adj.P.Val < 0.05 & abs(logFC) > log2(1.5))
Imputation can create results. A protein with three real values in one group and three imputed values in the other will look highly significant. Always check how many measured values stand behind each hit, and report the imputation method you used.
